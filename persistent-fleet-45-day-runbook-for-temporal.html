01

How to read this

One container, 45 days, every day accounted for. Each day states what fires, which Temporal primitive does it, which cash leak it addresses, and what happens to the money.

Day 1 is the arrival notice, not the discharge. The agent exists before the vessel does, which is the first thing most designs get wrong — by the time a box is discharged, two of the four free days are already committed.

Calendar dates are March–April 2026 and map to the worked example in the main project document. Charges use the published 2026 US West Coast tier ranges.

Notation

TIMERA durable workflow.sleep() or a wait_condition with a timeout. Survives crashes and deploys.
SIGNALAn external event delivered into a running — usually sleeping — workflow.
ACTIVITYWork with side effects or non-determinism. All LLM calls live here.
QUERYA side-effect-free read of live workflow state, for the control tower.
CHILDA child workflow spawned to own the next arc of the container's life.
SAGAA compensating action registered so a later failure can undo this step.
EVIDENCEA fact written to history with a source_document_id. Later becomes dispute material.
LEAK nnWhich of the seven cash leaks this day touches. See section 11.

Days where the agent is asleep are still shown. They matter: a sleeping day costs nothing, holds full state, and is the reason one agent per container is affordable at all.

02

The workflow cast

Four workflows. One parent that owns the container's identity for its whole life, and three children that each own exactly one clock.

WorkflowLivesOwnsEnds when
ContainerWorkflow
parent, entity workflow		Day 1 → 45+Identity, contract terms, the event log, all evidence, and the counterfactual ledgerEmpty returned and all disputes closed
DemurrageArc
child		Day 8 → 20The terminal clock. Availability polling, effective-LFD calculation, checkpoint loop, interventionsGate-out signal
DetentionArc
child		Day 20 → 31The equipment clock. Empty-return booking, restriction monitoring, chassis accrualEmpty accepted
DisputeArc
child, ABANDON		Day 40 → 90+One instance per invoice. Part 541 audit, evidence assembly, filing, follow-up cadence, settlementResolved, or escalated to a human

Why the split matters: the three clocks have different owners, different evidence, and different end conditions. Cramming them into one workflow produces a state machine you will spend the hackathon debugging. Splitting them means the detention agent keeps running long after the demurrage story is over — which is where a third of the money lives.

Why the parent holds the evidence

Each arc closes when its clock stops, but the dispute needs facts from all three arcs, filed weeks later. Evidence therefore accumulates on the parent and is passed down to DisputeArc at spawn time. Children report; the parent remembers.

# the four workflows, in one page
@workflow.defn
class ContainerWorkflow:
    @workflow.run
    async def run(self, c: ContainerInput):
        self.terms = await execute_activity(act.load_contract_terms, c.contract_id)

        # Day 1–7: exist, but do nothing expensive
        await workflow.wait_condition(lambda: self.discharged_at is not None)

        # Day 8–20
        dem = await execute_child_workflow(DemurrageArc.run, args=[c, self.terms])
        self.evidence += dem.evidence

        # Day 20–31
        det = await execute_child_workflow(DetentionArc.run, args=[c, self.terms])
        self.evidence += det.evidence

        # Day 40+: one dispute per invoice, each outliving this workflow
        while not self.closed:
            await workflow.wait_condition(lambda: self.pending_invoices)
            for inv in self.pending_invoices.pop_all():
                await start_child_workflow(
                    DisputeArc.run, args=[c.container_id, inv, self.terms, self.evidence],
                    id=f"dispute::{inv.invoice_id}",
                    parent_close_policy=ParentClosePolicy.ABANDON)
03

The burn curve

Cumulative cost across the 45 days, with and without agents. The two lines are identical until day 20 — because until gate-out, nothing was controllable. Everything after that is pure prevention.

Two things to notice. The slope steepens between days 17 and 20 — that is tier escalation, and it is why the last three days cost more than the first five. And the agent's line goes flat at exactly the moment the human process stops paying attention.

04

Phase 0 — days 1–7, pre-arrival

Nothing is at stake yet, and that is precisely why this phase exists. Everything the agent will need for the next six weeks gets loaded now, while it is cheap.

D1Feb 24Arrival notice — workflow starts$0

The carrier's arrival notice arrives. ContainerWorkflow is started with a deterministic ID of container::MSKU7481920, which makes the whole system idempotent — a duplicate notice cannot create a second agent.

The Watcher parses the service contract PDF and extracts free-time allowances, tier tables, weekend and holiday counting rules, and per-port exceptions into structured state. This is the single highest-leverage activity in the entire 45 days: it is the fact nobody currently has, and every later calculation depends on it.

Phase 1 — days 8–12, free time

The clock is running and nothing is billed yet. This is the only phase where cheap action beats expensive defence, and in our container it is also where the first leak silently opens.

D8Mar 3, 06:00Discharge — DemurrageArc spawned$0

The box comes off the vessel. The parent spawns DemurrageArc, which computes the nominal last free day at day 12 and begins polling for availability.

It does not yet trust that date. Nominal LFD is what the carrier will bill against; effective LFD is what the agent will argue.

D8–10Mar 3–5Ungrounded — the invisible loss$0 billed · 2 days lost

The container is buried in a stack. check_availability returns None on every poll, and each None is written to history with the terminal's own timestamp.

Nothing is billed today. But two of four free days have been consumed by the terminal's handling, and because the tier ladder is convex, those two days will eventually cost $900 at the far end. The most expensive days of this container are being created right now, and no human process notices.

When availability finally lands, the agent shifts the effective LFD by the measured gap and stamps the whole window as cited evidence.

D9Mar 4CET exam hold placed$0

Customs places a Contraband Enforcement Team hold. The container is routed to a Centralised Examination Station, unloaded piece by piece, at the importer's expense.

The signal wakes the sleeping workflow. Two things happen, and the second is the counter-intuitive one: the agent records the hold as evidence, then suppresses all intervention. Spending $340 on an expedited trucker for a container that is legally immovable is pure waste. A naive agent would fire every countermeasure it has.

Phase 2 — days 13–20, demurrage accruing

Eight billed days, $4,100 across two companies, and not one day attributable to the importer. The agent's job here is not to stop the bleeding — it cannot — but to make every day of it provable.

D13–16Mar 8–11Exam continues, charges compound$1,550 cumulative

Demurrage and storage now run together. The agent wakes daily, reassesses, and finds the same blocked state. Each wake writes one line: the hold is still open, checked at this timestamp, against this customs reference.

That daily record is what a human process cannot produce five weeks later. It is not clever — it is just continuous, which is exactly the thing humans cannot be across four hundred containers.

D18–20Mar 13–15No appointment slots — the expensive days$4,100 cumulative

The terminal's appointment system has nothing. The Negotiator scans continuously rather than trying once a day — a real operational practice, and here it doubles as an evidence machine.

Every failed attempt is written to history with a timestamp. This is the single most valuable by-product in the entire system. These three days cost $1,225 — nearly half the demurrage bill — and they are the strongest claim available, because the billing party's own invoice will certify that its performance did not contribute to the charge.

The saga matters here too. The agent books drayage first, then tries for a slot. No slot means the drayage booking must be unwound, or the importer eats a no-show fee that the agent itself created.

D20Mar 15, 14:00Gate-out — the clocks swap$4,100 locked in

A slot opens in the afternoon. The box leaves. DemurrageArc closes and returns its evidence bundle to the parent; DetentionArc is spawned in the same step.

This is the moment the human process stops paying attention and the agent does not. Everything that follows — a third of the total bill — exists purely because nobody watches the second clock.

Phase 3 — days 20–31, detention

The only phase where the agent changes the outcome rather than documenting it. Everything here is prevention, and prevention is worth more per dollar than recovery because it carries no dispute risk at all.

D21Mar 16Empty return booked — the decisive action−$1,485 avoided

One day after gate-out, before the cargo is even unloaded, the Strategist prices the second clock. Its options table looks like this:

OptionCostP(success)Expected saving
Do nothing, return when convenient$0—$0
Pre-book return slot for D23$00.90$1,485

The winning move costs nothing. It is not a clever optimisation — it is simply the consequence of somebody, or something, still watching. The learning store contributes the decisive prior: this depot has issued return restrictions three times in the last ninety days, so slack is expensive here.

D24–29Mar 19–24Carrier return restriction — the near missavoided by 1 day

The carrier announces it will not accept empties at this depot. In the unmanaged timeline this is where $1,485 is born: the trucker drives out, is turned away, and parks the box in his yard while detention and chassis both accrue.

Our container went back yesterday. The agent still ingests the advisory, matches it to the depot, and files it as a near miss — a labelled training signal for the learning store, which raises the return-urgency prior for this depot on every future container.

This is worth showing on stage. The agent did not predict the restriction. It bought slack cheaply because slack was free, and the restriction validated the choice.

Phase 4 — days 32–45, dispute

The container has been gone for two weeks. The agent is still working, and this is the phase no session-shaped system can reach at all.

D33Mar 28Case pre-assembled, before any invoice exists$0

The Case Builder assembles the full chronology from the parent's evidence bundle while the invoice is still weeks away. Every claim carries a source_document_id; anything without one is dropped at assembly time, not argued about in a prompt.

The draft argues three things: the effective LFD should have been day 14, not day 12; days 18–20 were caused by terminal capacity; and days 13–17 were a period during which the importer was legally forbidden to act.

D40Apr 4Demurrage and storage invoices arrive$4,100 billed

Two invoices from two companies. Each spawns its own DisputeArc with ParentClosePolicy.ABANDON, so each can run for months independently of everything else.

The Auditor recomputes from first principles and runs the regulatory checklist. In this container it finds a missing certification field on the carrier invoice — the statement that the billing party's own performance did not contribute to the charge. Under the FMC rules, omitting required content eliminates the obligation to pay that charge.

That single check, which takes an LLM about eight seconds, is worth more than every clever forecast in the system.

Time-skipping test plan

Temporal's time-skipping environment runs all 45 days in about a second of wall clock. Write these as tests first; the demo is then just the test with a UI attached.

async def test_full_lifecycle(env: WorkflowEnvironment):
    handle = await env.client.start_workflow(ContainerWorkflow.run, container, ...)

    # D8 — discharge
    await env.sleep(timedelta(days=7))
    await handle.signal(ContainerWorkflow.discharged, discharge_event)

    # D9 — hold placed; assert the agent does NOT spend
    await env.sleep(timedelta(days=1))
    await handle.signal(ContainerWorkflow.hold_placed, cet_hold)
    state = await handle.query(ContainerWorkflow.state)
    assert state["intervention_suppressed"] is True
    assert state["spend_usd"] == 0        # the key anti-waste assertion

    # D10 — availability lands late; effective LFD must shift
    await env.sleep(timedelta(days=1))
    assert state["effective_lfd"] == day(14)   # not day(12)

    # D20 — gate out spawns the detention arc
    await env.sleep(timedelta(days=10))
    await handle.signal(ContainerWorkflow.gate_out, gate_event)

    # D21 — the decisive assertion of the whole system
    await env.sleep(timedelta(days=1))
    det = env.client.get_workflow_handle(f"detention::{cid}")
    assert (await det.query("state"))["return_slot"] is not None

    # D24 — restriction arrives after the box is already back
    await env.sleep(timedelta(days=3))
    await det.signal("carrier_advisory", restriction)
    assert (await det.query("state"))["detention_days"] == 0

    # D40 — non-compliant invoice must be caught
    await env.sleep(timedelta(days=16))
    await handle.signal(ContainerWorkflow.invoice_received, invoice_missing_cert)
    dispute = env.client.get_workflow_handle(f"dispute::{inv_id}")
    assert "certification_absent" in (await dispute.query("findings"))

Build order

Build in this sequence and you have a demoable slice after step 3.

  1. The spine. ContainerWorkflow plus the pre-arrival wait and DemurrageArc. Stub every activity. Prove a 45-day sleep works under time-skipping before writing a single prompt.
  2. The clocks. Three independent allowances, tier tables, effective-LFD calculation. Pure logic, fully unit-testable, no LLM involved.
  3. The decisive path. Gate-out → DetentionArc → empty-return booking. This is $1,485 of the story and the clearest demo beat. Get it working before anything else that involves reasoning.
  4. The evidence machine. Availability polls and slot scans writing cited failures to history. Cheap to build, and it is what makes the dispute possible later.
  5. The Strategist. The first real LLM activity: costed options plus a stored counterfactual.
  6. The saga and the approval gate. Compensation on the booking path, spend cap parking on a signal.
  7. The Auditor. Part 541 checklist plus tier and weekend recompute. Highest value per line of code in the entire system.
  8. The Case Builder and DisputeArc. Cited chronology, filing, follow-up cadence.
  9. The control tower. Fleet map from search attributes, per-container reasoning trace from queries, event history viewer beside it.
  10. The synthetic fleet. 200 containers with realistic distributions, plus the agents-on/agents-off toggle.
Sequencing trap

The temptation is to start with the LLM, because that is the interesting part. Do not. If the spine cannot sleep 45 days and survive a worker restart, no amount of good reasoning saves the demo — and the spine is a day's work, while debugging it under time pressure is not.
