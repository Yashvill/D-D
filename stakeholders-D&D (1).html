01

Executive summary

The obvious reading of this project is that importers win and carriers lose. That reading is wrong, and getting it right is what makes the product sellable rather than merely clever.

Demurrage and detention exist to do one thing: get containers moving and equipment back in circulation. When our agent returns an empty container eight days earlier than the unmanaged process would have, the carrier gets exactly what the charge was designed to buy. It loses the penalty and gains the asset. Prevention is aligned with the party charging for it.

The dispute half is genuinely adversarial, but narrowly so. What gets contested are charges that are either non-compliant with federal invoice requirements or caused by the billing party's own performance failure. Carriers already concede roughly a fifth of what they bill. Structured, cited claims are cheaper for them to adjudicate than the vague, angry, evidence-free emails they get today.

The owner of the process is the facilitator — the freight forwarder or NVOCC. They sit at the only point in the chain where the contract, the event feed, the terminal login, the drayage relationship and the invoice all pass through the same hands. They also feel the pain first, because carriers debit their account before they have collected from the shipper.

The four reasons

They feel it first, and in cash. Carriers debit D&D from the facilitator's account before the facilitator has collected from the shipper. On a high-volume lane this opens a working-capital gap that compounds month over month. Pain that hits cash gets budget faster than pain that hits the profit line at quarter end.

They hold every input. The service contract, the EDI feed, the terminal portal credentials, the drayage relationship, the customs broker thread, and the invoice — all of it passes through the same organisation. The importer holds fragments. The trucker holds a slice.

Volume makes the agent worth building. One facilitator integration covers thousands of containers a year across dozens of shippers. One importer integration covers hundreds.

They already own the operational muscle. Ops staff, dispatcher relationships, portal logins, a dispute process however threadbare. You are upgrading a function that exists rather than creating one from nothing.

The regulatory fit

US rules give NVOCCs their own timing regime and an explicit mechanism to pass a customer's dispute upstream, with additional time granted for doing so. The intermediary role your facilitator plays is written into the regulation. That is unusual, and it is worth a slide.

04

The value split

Everything in this document depends on one distinction. Say it early and say it clearly, because it is what stops the project being read as an attack on carriers.

Half one — aligned

Prevention

The agent moves the box faster and returns the equipment sooner. The carrier loses a penalty it never wanted to earn and gains an asset it did want back. The terminal frees a slot. The importer pays less.

Nobody loses anything they were trying to keep. This half is roughly 35–40% of the value and it is entirely non-adversarial.

Half two — contested

Audit and dispute

The agent contests charges that are non-compliant with invoice rules, or caused by the billing party's own performance. Money moves from carrier to shipper.

This half is adversarial — but it is narrow, evidence-based, and it uses a federal mechanism built for exactly this. Carriers already waive about a fifth of billings; the fight is over whether a claim gets made, not whether the system permits it.

The practical consequence: sell the halves separately. Prevention is an operational service the facilitator markets to shippers and can discuss openly with carriers. Audit and dispute is a financial recovery function that lives with the controller. Some organisations will buy one and not the other, and the architecture supports either alone.

It also changes how you present the project. "We reduce penalty spend by making the supply chain do what the penalty was designed to encourage" is a sentence you can say in a room containing a carrier representative. "We help importers fight carriers" is not.

06

The carrier

Loses penalty, gains asset

Ocean carrier / steamship line

+8 equipment days

This is the section that decides whether the project reads as adversarial. Take it slowly.

A container earns money only while it is moving. The stated purpose of demurrage and detention is to get boxes out of terminals and equipment back into circulation. In our worked example the agent returns the empty on day 23 instead of day 31 — eight days of a capital asset put back into service that the carrier would otherwise have spent chasing.

The carrier loses $1,485 it did not want to earn, and gains eight days of the thing it actually wanted. Across a fleet that is a container-turn improvement, and container turns are how liner economics work.

07

The terminal and port

Loses storage, gains fluidity

Marine terminal operator

no-shows down

The terminal's scarce resource is not money, it is space and slots. Storage revenue is a by-product of congestion, and congestion is the thing that degrades every other move in the yard.

The strongest terminal benefit here is specific and often overlooked: appointment no-shows. Human dispatchers book defensively — grabbing slots they may not be able to use, because slots are scarce and holding one costs nothing. Every unused slot is capacity destroyed on a day when capacity was rationed.

Our agent cannot do that. The saga pattern requires a compensating action for every booking, so a slot the agent cannot use is released automatically rather than abandoned. That is a direct, mechanical improvement to the terminal's effective capacity, and it comes for free out of a design decision made for a completely different reason.

The port authority, one level up, cares about none of the revenue and all of the fluidity. Dwell time is a headline performance metric for a port complex, and this reduces it. That makes the port the easiest stakeholder in the entire chain to win over.

08

The drayage carrier

Pure upside

Drayage / trucking provider

wasted trips eliminated

The drayage carrier is the party that loses most and complains least, because they are the ones physically absorbing everyone else's information failure. They drive to a depot that will not accept the box. They hold a chassis they cannot release. They burn driver hours in a queue for an appointment that was never going to work.

They also have no visibility into any of the clocks. Nobody tells a trucker that a container is two days from the top tier, or that a return restriction was announced yesterday.

Financial improvements

Wasted trips eliminated, no-show fees avoided, chassis per diem shortened, and driver hours redirected to revenue moves. There is no offsetting loss anywhere in their P&L — this is the one party in the chain for whom the project is unambiguously positive.

Worth noting commercially: a facilitator whose agents give truckers ranked, pre-booked, non-wasted work becomes a preferred customer in a capacity-constrained market. That is a real operational advantage in a tight drayage market, and it costs nothing to provide.

2. Working capital

Carriers debit D&D before the facilitator collects from the shipper. Every prevented charge is a debit that never happens, and every recovery closes an open gap. On a high-volume lane this is the difference between funding someone else's penalty and not — and it is the argument that gets a CFO's attention faster than the savings figure.

3. Differentiation and retention

A facilitator who can show a shipper "we prevented $18,000 and recovered $9,000 for you this quarter, here is the container-by-container ledger" has a retention weapon that competitors cannot match with a rate card. D&D management moves from a hidden cost the forwarder absorbs to a visible service they sell.

4. Operating leverage

The ops team currently spends roughly two hours per affected container on reconciliation and dispute assembly — around 1,440 hours a year at this volume. The agent does not replace the ops team; it removes the archaeology, so the same headcount handles more containers and spends its time on exceptions rather than reconstruction.

5. Procurement leverage at renewal

After two quarters the learning store knows each carrier's nominal free time versus its effective free time at each port. Free time is a negotiable contract term, and on a high-volume lane two extra days can be worth more than a rate reduction. That is a permanent structural gain that outlasts the software.

The obligation that comes with ownership

The facilitator is both billed party and billing party. Recoveries must be visible to the shipper, or the retention weapon becomes a liability. Section 13 sets out three defensible models — pick one and publish it.

10

Where interests genuinely conflict

Read this section before pitching to anyone in a shipping line. A pitch that pretends everybody wins equally is not credible, and a judge will find the seam faster than you can paper over it.

1. Carriers lose penalty revenue. Full stop.

D&D is roughly $3 billion a year from nine carriers in US trades alone. Prevention removes revenue they currently book. There is no version of this where that is not true.

What is true alongside it: the revenue is a penalty, not a service line, and it is revenue they publicly justify as an incentive rather than a profit centre. Taking a company at its word is a defensible position.

2. Terminals lose storage revenue

Same structure, smaller number, and offset more cleanly by capacity gains — a terminal that runs at capacity converts freed slots into throughput. Port authorities, who measure dwell rather than storage revenue, are net winners.

3. Systemic repricing

If everybody adopted this, D&D revenue would fall and carriers would recover it in base freight. That is probably correct, and it should be said out loud.

The counter is not that the money disappears — it is that the money becomes a negotiated, predictable rate instead of an unpredictable penalty landing five weeks late on a delay you did not cause. That is a better market for planning, budgeting and cash management even if the total is unchanged.

4. Data access

The genuine operational risk. A carrier or terminal could restrict portal or API access to a tool it sees as adversarial.

The mitigation is structural: almost everything the agent needs is data the facilitator already owns or is entitled to receive — their own service contract, EDI messages already flowing to them, gate receipts and EIRs from their own truckers, and the invoices themselves. You do not need carrier goodwill to audit an invoice. Prevention benefits from good access; audit does not require it.

5. Contract repricing at renewal

Carriers can shorten free time or tighten dispute language for accounts that dispute heavily. Real, and worth naming before a judge does. The counter is that the learning store gives the facilitator better renewal data than they have ever had — and free time that is nominally four days but effectively two is a fact worth knowing at the table regardless of who is disputing what.

The framing that survives a hostile room

Never "beat the carriers." Always: pay what you owe, not what you don't. Prevent the charges that should not happen, pay the ones you earned, contest the ones that are non-compliant or caused by the biller. Every clause of that is defensible in front of a shipping line.

13

Commercial model

The facilitator is both the billed party and the billing party. When the agent recovers $3,000, whose money is it? Answer this before anyone asks, because it is the first thing a logistics person will notice.

ModelHow it worksBest whenRisk
Pass through, charge for the serviceRecovery goes to the shipper. Facilitator earns a per-container subscription or a management fee.The facilitator wants D&D management as a differentiator and a retention toolSlower payback; needs a sales motion
Keep what was absorbedWhere the facilitator ate the charge rather than re-billing it, recovery is theirs outright.Relationship accounts where charges were routinely absorbedRequires clean records of what was absorbed
Split by causePrevention savings go to whoever would have paid; audit findings on non-compliant invoices go to whoever fronted the cash.Mixed books with both absorbed and passed-through chargesMore complex to explain

The recommendation is the first model. It turns D&D management into something the facilitator sells rather than something it hides, and it aligns the agent's incentives with the shipper's. A facilitator caught quietly pocketing recoveries has a lawsuit; one publishing a quarterly ledger has a renewal.

Whichever model is chosen, make the ledger visible to the shipper, container by container, with prevented and recovered shown separately. Transparency here is not a courtesy — it is the mechanism that converts savings into retention.

Pricing shapes worth testing

  • Per-container subscription. Predictable for both sides; scales with volume rather than with dysfunction.
  • Success fee on recoveries. Easy to sell, but it pays the vendor more when the supply chain performs worse — a bad incentive to design in.
  • Hybrid. A low base fee covering prevention, plus a success share on disputes only. Aligns incentives on the half where alignment matters.

The hybrid is the honest answer. Prevention should never be priced on failure.

Shadow mode first is non-negotiable. At launch the agent has no priors — no idea which depots issue restrictions, how far ahead this terminal releases slots, or which arguments win against which carrier. Three weeks of observation buys the calibration that earns the right to act, and it costs nothing but time.

What the POC must prove

  1. The clocks are computable. Three allowances, per carrier and terminal, extracted from contracts and tariffs and reconciled against real invoices.
  2. Evidence accumulates by itself. Availability gaps and appointment failures captured with timestamps, without anyone remembering to.
  3. The empty-return path fires. The single highest-value action, and the one that needs no dispute to realise.
  4. Invoice audit finds real defects. Run the regulatory checklist against 90 days of genuine invoices and count the hits. This is the number that will surprise the room.
  5. The counterfactual holds up. Predictions scored against outcomes, with the misses shown.

"Give us one lane and ninety days. We will run in shadow for three weeks and show you what you would have saved. Then we will act on the free wins only. 15

Objections, by stakeholder

From the facilitator

"Our shippers will ask why we weren't doing this already." Because it was not possible to do at volume. The honest answer builds trust: nobody could hold four hundred containers' worth of context for six weeks each. Frame the launch as a capability, not an apology.

"Will this damage our carrier relationships?" The prevention half improves them. The dispute half is a channel the carrier already operates and already concedes into. What damages relationships is surprise escalation, which this reduces.

"What if the agent books something wrong?" Spend caps bound the exposure, compensation undoes side effects, and phase one is shadow mode. The first six weeks contain no autonomous spending at all.

From the importer

"How do I know you're passing on the recoveries?" The container-by-container ledger, published quarterly, with prevented and recovered shown separately. Pick the pass-through model and this objection disappears.

"Is disputing going to get us blacklisted?" Disputing is a mechanism the regulation establishes and the carrier already operates. What changes is the quality of the claim, not the fact of it.

From the carrier

"You are just trying to avoid legitimate charges." We are avoiding the delays that generate them, which is what the charge was designed to do. Where we dispute, we dispute charges that are non-compliant or caused by your own performance — and we bring documents rather than assertions.

"This will flood us with disputes." Volume up, cost per dispute down. A cited claim naming a specific defect is faster to adjudicate than a reconstructed argument, and defects surfaced privately do not become formal complaints.

From the terminal

"Automated scanning will hammer our appointment system." Rate limiting is enforced at the worker level across the whole fleet, not per container. And the same design releases slots it cannot use — no-show reduction is a direct gain to your capacity.

From a judge

"Isn't this zero-sum?" Roughly 40% of the value is prevention, which is aligned with the party charging for it. The remaining 60% is contested, but it is contested under a federal mechanism built for the purpose, against charges the carriers themselves already waive at about a fifth of billings.

The one metric that governs all of them

Prediction calibration. Predicted charges against realised charges, with the misses shown rather than hidden. It is the number that earns the agent the right to act autonomously, and it is the number that keeps every other figure in this document honest.

A dashboard that only ever reports wins is a dashboard nobody in logistics will believe. Show the cases where the agent spent money that did not need spending, and where it predicted a charge that never came. That is what separates a measured system from a marketed one.

The closing line

Every party in this chain is blind to the thing that causes their own cost. The agent does not automate anyone's job — it supplies the missing input that made everyone's job impossible to do well. That is why the value is not zero-sum, and it is why the facilitator, sitting where all five views overlap, is the only party who can build it.
