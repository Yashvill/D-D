01

Executive summary

Every shipping container has a lifespan of weeks. Every AI agent has a lifespan of one conversation. That mismatch is why billions of dollars in avoidable penalty fees get paid every year — and it is exactly the mismatch Temporal closes.

$15.4 billion
Demurrage and detention collected by just nine ocean carriers in US trades, April 2020 to March 2025.
Source: Federal Maritime Commission quarterly data collection [1]

That is nine carriers, one country, five years. It is not a pandemic artefact either — the FMC's own index shows amounts billed and collected peaking again in Q4 2024, some 85% above the Q2 2020 baseline. [1]

When a container arrives at a port, two clocks start. One counts the days the box sits in the terminal (demurrage); the other counts the days you keep the carrier's equipment after collecting it (detention). Overrun either and the charges escalate daily. At Los Angeles and Long Beach in 2026, free time has compressed to two to four days at many terminals, and tiered rates run roughly $200 per day at the first tier up to $500 per day at the worst. [5]

Here is the number that makes this a business, not a complaint. Over 2020–2022 those nine carriers billed about $8.9 billion and collected about $6.9 billion. [4] Roughly a fifth of what gets billed never gets paid — because somebody disputed it and won. The disputes work. Almost nobody makes them.

They don't, for one reason: the job requires continuous attention across a 24-to-60 day window, joining a contract PDF to a live event stream to an invoice that arrives five weeks later, inside a 30-day dispute window. Humans cannot hold ten thousand of those in working memory at once. Neither can a chatbot, which forgets everything the moment the session ends.

Persistent Fleet gives every container its own agent, and gives that agent a lifespan that matches the container's. Built on Temporal, each agent sleeps for days, wakes on real-world events, prices its options, acts within a bounded mandate, escalates to a human when spend crosses a threshold, and — after the box is long gone — assembles and prosecutes the dispute inside the regulatory deadline. It survives crashes, deploys and restarts, because the workflow is the memory.

02

What we are solving

The problem in one paragraph

Importers are billed penalty charges for container delays they frequently did not cause and could not have prevented. A large share of those charges are either avoidable with three days' notice or disputable under US federal rules. Almost none are avoided or disputed, because doing so requires one continuous thread of attention stretching across six weeks and four disconnected systems — and no such thread exists in any importer's operation today.

Why it is not a data problem

Every fact needed to win already exists somewhere. The free time is in the service contract. The discharge time is in an EDI 315. The availability time is in a terminal portal. The exam window is in a customs status feed. The return restriction is in an advisory email. The charge is on an invoice.

It is an attention problem. Nothing joins those six things together, at the right moment, for the right container, and holds them until the invoice lands five weeks later.

Why agentic AI

The work is judgement-heavy on messy inputs. Reading free-time clauses out of a contract PDF. Deciding whether a carrier advisory applies to this container at this depot. Weighing a $340 expedite against a $1,485 risk. Drafting a dispute that cites its own evidence and matches the specific regulatory defect in the invoice. None of these are rules you write once.

Why Temporal, specifically

Apply the honest test: could you build this with a cron job and a queue? No — and not marginally. The process sleeps for days at a time and must survive deploys. It parks indefinitely waiting for a human to approve a spend in Slack. It takes real actions in real carrier systems that must be undone when customs intervenes an hour later. It must produce a permanent, replayable record of what the agent knew and when it knew it — because under the FMC rules, that record is the dispute evidence.

Each of those is a distributed-systems project on its own. Temporal makes them ordinary.

The framing to lead with

Today's AI agents are session-shaped: they wake, think, and die. The physical world is not session-shaped — a container spends 45 days in it. Temporal lets us give every container an agent that lives as long as the container's journey does.

03

Glossary of terms

Everything below appears later in the document. The three charges in the first block are the ones people most often confuse, and that confusion is itself a source of loss.

The charges

Demurrage
Charged when a full container sits inside the terminal past its free time. You are being penalised for occupying yard space. On imports the clock runs from discharge (or from when the box is made available) until you collect it. Mental model: your cargo in their yard.
Detentiona.k.a. per diem
Charged by the carrier when you hold their container outside the terminal past its free time — from gate-out until you return the empty to the designated depot. Mental model: their box in your yard.
Terminal storage
Charged by the terminal operator — a different company from the carrier — for the same yard time demurrage covers. Its own free-time allowance, its own counting rules. A container can legitimately incur demurrage and storage on the same day.
Chassis per diem
A separate daily charge for the wheeled frame the container sits on, typically $35–$50 per day. [5] Forgotten in almost every cost estimate, and it accrues right through an empty-return restriction.

The clock

Free time
Days granted before charges begin. Set in the service contract for demurrage and detention, in the terminal tariff for storage. Frequently different for each, and different per port.
Last free dayLFD
The final day before charges accrue. The single most important date in the process, and the one most often computed wrongly.
Tier table
The escalating rate schedule. Escalation is why small delays produce disproportionate bills — the last two days of a delay can cost more than the first five.
Effective LFD
Our term, not the industry's. The last free day adjusted for days the container was not actually available for collection. The gap between nominal and effective LFD is pure recoverable money.

Events and documents

Discharge
The container is lifted off the vessel. Nominally starts the demurrage clock.
Availabilitygrounded / appointable
The moment the box can actually be collected — grounded, released, and bookable. Often one to three days after discharge.
Gate-out
The container leaves the terminal. Stops demurrage and starts detention in the same instant.
Empty return
The emptied box is accepted back at the depot. Arriving is not enough — a turned-away trucker does not stop the clock.
CET examA-TCET, intensive
A Contraband Enforcement Team examination. The container is moved to a Centralised Examination Station and physically unloaded. Duration commonly runs one to two weeks or more, and the importer bears the cost. [8][9]
VACIS exam
A non-intrusive X-ray or gamma scan of the whole container. The most common ocean exam type and usually the quickest to clear. [9]
Empty return restriction
The carrier announces it will not accept empties at a given depot. Detention typically keeps accruing anyway.
Equipment interchange receiptEIR
The document recording custody transfer at a gate. Primary dispute evidence — it timestamps exactly when the box changed hands.
EDI 315 / 322
Standard electronic messages carrying container status events (315) and terminal operations events (322). The main machine-readable feed.

Parties and operations

CarrierVOCC / steamship line
Owns the container and the contract with you. Bills demurrage and detention.
Terminal operatorMTO
Runs the port facility. Owns the yard and the appointment system. Bills storage.
NVOCC / forwarder
An intermediary that contracts with the carrier and re-bills you. Sits in the middle of the dispute chain and gets its own timing rules under the FMC regime.
Consignee
The party receiving the cargo. Usually the one who ends up paying.
Drayage
Short-haul trucking between terminal and warehouse or depot.
Merchant haulage
You arrange your own trucking and transact with the terminal directly, so storage charges land on you separately.
Carrier haulage
The carrier arranges the inland move; terminal charges reach you consolidated.
Off-dock yard
A depot outside the terminal. Moving a box there stops the terminal clock at the cost of an extra drayage leg later.

Technical terms

Workflow
In Temporal, a durable function whose entire execution is recorded and replayable. Survives process crashes and redeploys.
Activity
A unit of work called by a workflow. Anything non-deterministic — network calls, LLM calls, clock reads — must live here.
Signal
An asynchronous message delivered into a running workflow from outside. How real-world events reach a sleeping agent.
Query
A synchronous, side-effect-free read of a running workflow's state. How the dashboard sees what each agent is thinking.
Saga
A pattern where each completed step has a compensating action that undoes it if a later step fails.
Entity workflow
One long-lived workflow instance per real-world object — here, one per container. The architectural centrepiece.
Determinism
The requirement that workflow code produce identical results on replay. It is why the agent's reasoning lives in activities and never in the workflow itself.
04

How the money works

Real published rates, current for 2026 where available. Everything in this section is sourced; see section 23.

Why the charge exists at all

A container only earns money while it is moving. A box making eight round trips a year earns twice what a box making four earns, off the same purchase price. Every idle day is a day that asset generates nothing — and it is idle in a terminal with finite capacity, slowing every other move around it. Escalating penalties are how the industry prices that congestion.

But note that pure cost recovery does not escalate. Tiered escalation is a price signal, which is why the same mechanism punishes delays you could not possibly control.

Why the carrier bills you for a yard it does not own

Because you have no contract with the terminal — you have a bill of lading with the carrier, and the carrier has a terminal services agreement with the terminal. Money flows along contracts, not along real estate. The terminal bills the carrier for overstay; the carrier bills you, at a marked-up and escalating rate that also prices its own equipment downtime.

Under merchant haulage you also transact with the terminal directly, so its storage tariff lands on you separately. That is why two invoices for overlapping days from two companies is normal — and why genuine double-billing hides inside a legitimate-looking overlap.

What that adds up to

Container xChange's benchmark measures accumulated D&D two weeks after discharge. In 2023 the six most expensive US ports — New York, Oakland, Los Angeles, Savannah, Long Beach and Houston — averaged about $2,008 per container at that fourteen-day mark, down from roughly $2,692 in 2022. [2][3] The same benchmark put average detention alone at about $123 seven days after gate-out and about $537 at fourteen days. [13]

For scale on the other axis, one industry estimate puts the global cost of D&D at roughly $22 billion a year across all trades. [14] Treat that as an estimate; the FMC's $15.4 billion is a hard number and covers only nine carriers in US trades. [1]

The compounding trap

Because rates are tiered, delay cost is convex. Two extra days at the start of a delay might cost $400. The same two days at the end of a nine-day delay cost $900 or more. This is why a $340 intervention on day three routinely beats a $1,500 charge on day nine — and why the intervention has to happen early, when nobody is paying attention yet.

05

The regulatory lever

This section is what makes the project a product rather than a dashboard. Since May 2024, US federal rules give importers a concrete, machine-checkable basis for refusing to pay — and almost nobody uses it, because checking requires evidence nobody kept.

The Ocean Shipping Reform Act of 2022 directed the Federal Maritime Commission to regulate D&D billing. The resulting final rule, 46 CFR Part 541, took effect on 28 May 2024. [15][16] In September 2025 the D.C. Circuit set aside exactly one section — 541.4, which had governed who may be invoiced — leaving the invoice-contents requirements, the issuance deadline and the dispute windows fully in force. [17]

The four rules that matter

1. Invoice within 30 days, or it is not payable

A billing party must issue a D&D invoice within 30 calendar days of the date the charge was last incurred. An NVOCC passing a charge through gets 30 days from the date it received the upstream invoice. Miss the deadline and the billed party has no obligation to pay. [15][16][18]

2. Missing required content voids the charge

Section 541.6 sets out five categories of mandatory invoice content — identifying, timing, rate, dispute, and certification information — amounting to roughly twenty numbered items depending on whether the shipment is an import or export. [19] Under 541.5, omitting any applicable required item eliminates the obligation to pay that charge. [20] This makes the first move in any dispute a line-by-line read of the invoice, which is exactly the kind of tedious, high-volume, rule-bound checking an agent does better than a person.

3. The certification clause

Every compliant US D&D invoice now carries the billing party's own written statement that the charges are consistent with FMC rules and that its own performance did not cause or contribute to the charge. [19][21]

Read that against our worked example. The terminal had no appointment slots for three days. The carrier refused the empty for six. In both cases the billing party's performance is precisely what caused the charge — and the billing party has certified in writing that it did not. That is not a sympathy argument; it is a documented contradiction, and it is the single strongest lever the Case Builder has.

4. Thirty days to dispute, thirty days to resolve

The billed party gets at least 30 calendar days from invoice issuance to request mitigation, refund or waiver. If a timely request is made, the billing party must attempt to resolve it within 30 days unless both agree otherwise. A Charge Complaint may be filed with the FMC at any point in the process. [15][18][22]

Why this changes the design

The dispute is winnable on documented facts and a deadline, not on relationship or negotiation skill. That makes it automatable — and it means the binding constraint is evidence captured contemporaneously, which is exactly what a durable agent produces as a side effect of simply running.

The number that proves disputes work

Over 2020–2022 the nine largest carriers in US trades billed approximately $8.9 billion in D&D and collected approximately $6.9 billion. [4] Roughly one dollar in five was billed and never collected — waived, mitigated or successfully disputed. The FMC's ongoing index tracks "waived" as its own series alongside billed and collected. [1]

So the mechanism works. The constraint is not whether carriers concede — they demonstrably do — it is that hardly anyone asks, because asking costs more staff time than the charge.

06

The 45 days today

This is the "before" picture, told slowly. Our container is a 40-foot high cube of garden furniture, Ningbo to Los Angeles, discharging at Pier 400 and delivering to a warehouse in Fontana. The importer is a mid-size retailer moving roughly 3,000 containers a year — big enough to have an ops team, small enough that the team is stretched.

Days −7 to 0 — before arrival

The arrival notice lands. Customs entry is filed in advance. The drayage carrier is on notice. Everything is fine.

The service contract that governs the next six weeks was signed months ago. It grants four free days at this port. It is a PDF in a shared drive. Nobody has opened it since signature, and nobody will open it again until after the invoice arrives.

Day 0 — discharge

The box comes off the vessel at 06:00. The demurrage clock starts. Nominal last free day: day 4.

Nobody records this date anywhere a system can act on. The ops team knows the container "arrived" and marks it as such.

Days 0–2 — the invisible loss

The container is in a stack, ungrounded and not appointable. No trucker could collect it if they tried. Two of the four free days are consumed by the terminal's handling before the importer has any ability to act.

This is recorded in the terminal portal. Nobody looks at the terminal portal on day 1.

Day 1 — customs hold

A CET exam hold is placed. The container is routed to a Centralised Examination Station. It is now legally and physically out of reach, and the importer pays for the privilege. [8][9]

The broker emails to say there's an exam. The email goes into a thread. Nobody logs the timestamp.

Day 5 — the clock starts, unnoticed

The last free day has passed. Demurrage now accrues daily on a container nobody is permitted to touch. Terminal storage, which had a shorter allowance, started accruing on day 4.

There is no alert. There is no dashboard. There is no person whose job it is to notice.

Days 5–9 — the exam

The exam runs nine days end to end, well inside the normal range. [9] Charges compound through two tier steps. Nobody can do anything, and — critically — nobody captures the fact that nobody could do anything.

Days 9–12 — no appointment slots

Customs releases. The drayage carrier tries to book a pickup and fails three times: the terminal's appointment system has no slots. A dispatcher grumbles in Slack. He does not screenshot the empty grid, because it does not occur to him that in five weeks that screenshot will be worth $1,225.

These are the most expensive days of the whole delay, because the tier ladder is at its top.

Day 12 — gate-out

The box finally leaves. Demurrage stops at eight billed days. Detention starts in the same instant. The ops team mentally files this container as done.

Day 14 — delivered and stripped

Cargo is unloaded at Fontana. From the consignee's point of view, finished. The empty sits on a chassis in the trucker's yard. Nobody books a return appointment, because nobody is tracking the second clock.

Days 16–21 — the empty return restriction

The carrier issues an advisory: it will not accept empties at that depot. The advisory goes to a shared inbox. The trucker drives out on day 17, is turned away, and drives back. Detention accrues. Chassis per diem accrues.

Had anyone returned the box on day 14 or 15, before the restriction began, this entire second charge would not exist.

Day 23 — empty accepted

Six billed detention days, eight billed chassis days.

Days 35–42 — the invoices

Three invoices arrive from two companies. Total $5,585. The ops team has handled roughly four hundred other containers since this one. Reconstructing what happened would take half a day, and the dispatcher who could confirm the appointment system was empty has moved to another account.

The regulatory clock is now running: 30 days to dispute. Nobody starts it.

Day 45 — payment

Finance pays. Not because anyone decided the charge was fair, but because the invoice is below the review threshold, and the review threshold exists because disputing costs more than paying.

The quiet variant

On some lanes the carrier holds cargo release pending settlement. The importer pays to free the goods, intending to dispute afterwards. Nothing captures that intent, no process carries it forward, and the protest quietly becomes acceptance.

09

Every place money leaks

The charge is not the leak. The leak is everywhere the organisation fails to prevent, contest, or even notice the charge. There are seven, and they compound.

LEAK012 days

The clock started before you could act

Mar 3 to 5, the box was buried in a stack, ungrounded and unappointable. Half of a four-day allowance was consumed by the terminal's own handling.

Almost nobody adjusts the last free day for this, because it needs the availability timestamp from a terminal portal that nobody checks on day two. Those two days push you two days deeper into the tier ladder — and the top tier is where the money is.

LEAK025 days

Billed for days you were forbidden to move

The CET exam accounts for five of the eight billed demurrage days. The container was at a Centralised Examination Station being unloaded piece by piece, at the importer's expense. [8][9] There was no action available.

A penalty that cannot change behaviour is not an incentive. Most importers pay anyway, because nobody assembled the exam timeline while it was fresh.

LEAK03$1,225

Billed for the terminal's own capacity failure

Three days lost to an appointment system with no slots — and because they land at the top of the tier ladder, those three days are $1,225, nearly half the demurrage bill.

This is the strongest available claim and the one most often lost, because it rests on evidence that decays: a screenshot of an empty grid on Mar 13. If the dispatcher did not capture it, the day is unprovable by Mar 20. Evidence perishes faster than the invoice arrives.

LEAK04$1,485

Detention on a box the carrier refused to take back

The entire second half of the bill exists because nobody watched the second clock. Had the empty gone back on Mar 17, before the restriction began on Mar 19, there would be no detention and no chassis charge.

When the restriction did bite, the consignee was charged for holding equipment they had actively tried to return — while the carrier's invoice certified that its own performance did not contribute to the charge. [19]

LEAK05silent

Nobody knew the deadline until it had passed

The contracted free time sat in a PDF. The discharge event sat in an EDI 315. Nothing joined them. There was no moment on Mar 5 when a system said two days left, no appointment booked, exam still open.

Three separate free-time allowances expired on three different days and not one of them triggered anything.

LEAK06all of it

The dispute window closed before the dispute started

Invoices arrive five to six weeks after discharge. Federal rules give the billed party at least 30 days to request mitigation, refund or waiver. [15][22] Meanwhile the team has moved on to four hundred other containers and reconstructing this one takes half a day.

So finance pays anything below a review threshold, and every leak above becomes permanent — regardless of merit, and regardless of whether the invoice even complied with Part 541.

LEAK07unmeasured

Paid under duress, then never revisited

Release of the cargo is often withheld pending settlement. The importer pays to free the goods, intending to dispute afterwards. Nothing captures that intent, no process carries it forward, and the protest quietly becomes acceptance.

The scoreboard

Of $5,585 billed on one ordinary container, roughly $4,635 was either preventable or strongly defensible. What gets paid today is all of it — not because anyone decided to, but because no single person ever held the contract terms, the event stream and the invoice in their head at the same time.

The line for the slide

How the agents work

Five narrow agents rather than one general one. Narrow agents are easier to prompt, easier to evaluate, easier to constrain, and easier to explain in thirty seconds each.

Watcher — ingest and normalise

Turns a mess of formats into one clean event model: EDI 315 and 322 messages, vessel schedules, terminal portal scrapes, customs status codes, carrier advisory emails, gate receipts, equipment interchange receipts and invoice PDFs.

Three jobs here are genuinely hard and genuinely agentic:

  • Resolving identity. The same container appears as MSKU7481920, MSKU 748192-0 and "the Ningbo box" across four systems.
  • Extracting terms. Pulling free-time allowances, tier tables, weekend rules and per-port exceptions out of a contract written as prose.
  • Classifying unstructured comms. Reading a carrier advisory and deciding whether it affects this container at this depot — the difference between leak 04 happening and not.

It also flags anomalies: an event contradicting an earlier one, or an expected event that never arrived.

Strategist — predict and price

Computes the effective LFD from actual availability rather than nominal discharge. Forecasts probability of gate-out before the LFD given chassis supply, appointment density, trucker capacity and terminal congestion. Then produces a costed options table — not a recommendation, a menu with expected values.

OptionCostP(success)Expected savingNote
Do nothing$0—$0Baseline; container enters tier 3
Expedite drayage$3400.72$1,190Premium trucker with slot access
Move to off-dock yard$2200.85$980Stops terminal clock; adds a leg later
Pre-book empty return$00.90$1,485Highest expected value in this case
Request free-time extension$00.35$650Carrier-dependent

The crucial output is the counterfactual: what the agent predicts happens if it does nothing. Stored, then checked against reality — which is what makes the savings claim auditable rather than assertable.

Negotiator — act on the world

Books and rebooks drayage. Scans for terminal appointments continuously, which is a real practice — some 3PLs already run 24/7 slot-scanning to catch releases at off-peak hours. [10] Books the empty return early. Requests free-time extensions with a reasoned case. Coordinates chassis. Drafts and sends messages, then manages follow-up over weeks.

The bounded mandate is the whole game: hard spend caps, no contract acceptance, mandatory escalation on anomalies, and a defined settlement band it may accept without asking.

Auditor — validate the invoice

Underrated, and the most legally grounded of the five. On invoice receipt it runs a checklist derived directly from 46 CFR Part 541:

CheckBasisIf it fails
Issued within 30 days of last charge?541.7(a)No obligation to pay [16][18]
All required identifying, timing, rate, dispute and certification fields present?541.6, 541.5No obligation to pay that charge [19][20]
Certification present that the billing party's performance did not contribute?541.6Contradiction with recorded evidence [19][21]
Tier table applied per contract?ContractRecompute and claim the difference
Weekend and holiday counting per contract?ContractRecompute
Same day billed twice under two labels?Contract / tariffClaim the overlap
Free time correct for this contract and port?ContractRecompute

A meaningful share of charges are simply wrong or non-compliant, independent of whether the underlying delay was justified. This is the cheapest money in the entire system.

Case builder — prosecute the dispute

Assembles the evidence chronology with a source document cited for every factual claim — no citation, no claim. Drafts the dispute letter, matched to the specific defect the Auditor found. Then runs the dispute as a long-lived process: submit inside the window, follow up on schedule, escalate through carrier tiers, evaluate partial settlement against expected recovery, accept within mandate or hand to a human. A Charge Complaint to the FMC remains available throughout. [22]

The learning loop

Per-port, per-carrier, per-terminal statistics on what works. Which arguments win against which carrier. How far ahead appointments genuinely need booking at each terminal. Which depots issue return restrictions and when. That feeds the Strategist's priors — and upward into procurement: this carrier's free time is nominally four days but effectively two at this port, so price the next contract accordingly. Free time is negotiable, and an extra two days on a high-volume lane can be worth more than a rate reduction. [23]

The workflow spine

Three workflows mirror the three real-world arcs. Nothing in workflow code may be non-deterministic — no LLM calls, no clock reads outside workflow.now(), no randomness, no I/O. All of that lives in activities.

The five pieces of code that carry the argument

1. Sleeping for days, waking on an event

A durable timer raced against a signal. The agent sleeps through the boring parts but wakes instantly when customs places a hold.

# race a multi-day timer against any incoming signal
try:
    await workflow.wait_condition(
        lambda: self.reassess_now or self.gated_out_at is not None,
        timeout=target - workflow.now(),
    )
    return True          # woke early — something happened
except asyncio.TimeoutError:
    return False         # reached the scheduled checkpoint

2. The approval gate

No polling loop, no state machine, no expiring job. The workflow parks for as long as the human takes.

if choice.requires_approval or choice.cost_usd > AUTO_APPROVE_LIMIT_USD:
    await workflow.execute_activity(act.notify_human, ...)
    await workflow.wait_condition(lambda: choice.action in self.approvals)

3. Compensation, so the agent leaves nothing broken behind

This is what stops an autonomous agent creating phantom bookings and no-show fees in real carrier systems. Note that a missing appointment slot raises rather than returning — it is not an error, it is evidence, recorded before the unwind.

compensations = []
try:
    booking = await workflow.execute_activity(act.book_drayage, ...)
    compensations.append((act.cancel_drayage, booking))

    slot = await workflow.execute_activity(act.reserve_appointment, ...)
    if slot is None:
        self.events.append(MilestoneEvent(kind="appointment_unavailable", ...))
        raise NoSlotAvailable()          # leak 03, captured as evidence
    compensations.append((act.release_appointment, slot))
except Exception:
    for fn, arg in reversed(compensations):
        await workflow.execute_activity(fn, arg, ...)

4. The handoff between clocks

Gate-out ends the demurrage arc and begins the detention arc. ABANDON is what lets the second clock outlive the first — and the second clock is where $1,485 of our worked example lives.

await workflow.start_child_workflow(
    DetentionWorkflow.run, args=[container, terms],
    id=f"detention::{container.container_id}",
    parent_close_policy=ParentClosePolicy.ABANDON,
)

5. The dashboard's read path

No projection table, no cache invalidation, no eventual consistency. The workflow is the read model.

@workflow.query
def state(self) -> dict:
    return {
        "effective_lfd": str(self.effective_lfd),
        "holds": sorted(self.holds),
        "awaiting_approval": self.pending_approval,
        "counterfactual_usd": self.counterfactual_usd,
    }
The determinism boundary

Workflow code replays identically forever, so it cannot contain an LLM call, a random number or a wall-clock read. The agent's reasoning goes inside activities; the workflow only orchestrates. Judges who know Temporal will probe this, and getting it right is the difference between "used Temporal" and "understood Temporal".

14

System architecture

No separate state store. Conventional agent architectures bolt a vector database on and call it memory. Here the workflow state is the memory — durable, queryable, and consistent with the audit log by construction, because they are the same thing.

Split task queues. LLM activities go to a reasoning queue with low concurrency and generous timeouts; portal scrapes go to a cheap I/O queue with aggressive retries. This also gives fleet-wide rate limiting against a carrier API for free, without coordination between workflows.

Workflows sleeping on timers consume no worker capacity. Concurrency is bounded by activity execution, not by how many agents are alive — which is why one agent per container is affordable at all.

Every event carries a source document identifier. This is not bookkeeping pedantry — under Part 541 it is what turns a log into a usable dispute, and it is the constraint that keeps the Case Builder honest.

16

Guardrails

An agent that spends money and sends messages into carrier systems needs bounds that are structural, not merely prompted. Every one of these is enforced in workflow code, where it can be audited, rather than in a system prompt, where it can be talked around.

  • Hard spend cap per action and per container. Anything above the auto-approve limit parks on a signal and waits, indefinitely.
  • No contract acceptance. The agent may request an extension; it may never agree to amended terms.
  • Bounded settlement authority. The Case Builder may accept an offer above a defined fraction of the claim and must escalate anything below.
  • Mandatory escalation on anomaly. Contradictory events, missing expected events, or a low-confidence assessment all route to a human.
  • Compensation is required, not optional. Any action with a real-world side effect must register a compensator before the next step runs.
  • No claim without a citation. The Case Builder cannot assert a fact with no source_document_id. Enforced at assembly time, not requested in a prompt.
  • Humans submit above threshold. The agent assembles and drafts; a person files. Nothing here changes who is legally responsible.
  • Full replayability. Every input to every decision is in event history, so any disputed action can be reconstructed exactly as the agent saw it.
The honest limitation

The agent's predictions are only as good as its priors, and at launch it has none. Early deployment should run in shadow mode — assessing and recommending without acting — until the counterfactual record shows its calls are calibrated. Say this out loud to judges; claiming otherwise invites the obvious question.

Proving the value

Judges are trained to distrust AI savings numbers, so metric design matters as much as the metric. Be the team that shows its error bars.

The counterfactual ledger

For every intervention the agent stores what it predicted would happen if it did nothing. Because the simulation keeps running, that prediction gets scored against what actually occurred. The dashboard therefore does not claim savings; it evidences them, and it shows the cases where the agent was wrong.

Demo script

Zoom into MSKU 748192-0. This is the most persuasive ten seconds available to you, because every judge has watched an LLM agent die and lose everything.

Questions judges will ask

Why not a cron job and a queue?

Because the process sleeps for days and must survive deploys, parks indefinitely on human approval, takes real actions that must be undone when customs intervenes an hour later, and must produce a permanent replayable record. Each is a distributed-systems project on its own.

Why not LangGraph or a standard agent framework?

Those manage reasoning within a session. Nothing here is within a session. The unit of work is 24 to 60 days and the agent is asleep for most of it. We use an LLM for reasoning and Temporal for lifespan.

How do you keep the workflow deterministic with an LLM in it?

We don't put the LLM in the workflow. Every model call is an activity, so its result is recorded in history and replayed rather than re-executed.

Isn't this just an alerting tool?

Alerting tells you the deadline is coming. This books the empty return, records the appointment failure as evidence, checks the invoice against federal requirements, and files the dispute inside the window. Two-thirds of the value in our worked example comes from actions, not notifications.

Can an AI file a dispute? Is that legally sound?

The agent assembles and drafts; a human submits above a threshold. Nothing changes who is legally responsible. The evidence chronology it produces is stronger than what a person assembles five weeks later, because it was captured contemporaneously.

What happens when the agent is wrong?

Spend caps bound the damage, compensation undoes side effects, and the counterfactual ledger surfaces the error rather than hiding it. Early deployment runs in shadow mode until calibration is demonstrated.

Ten thousand concurrent workflows — does that scale?

Workflows sleeping on timers consume no worker capacity. Concurrency is bounded by activity execution, not by how many agents are alive.

How do you deploy new agent logic without breaking live containers?

Temporal's versioning. Workflows in flight continue on the logic they started with; new ones pick up the new path.

What is the hardest part you have not solved?

Identity resolution across carrier, terminal and customs systems, and extracting free-time terms reliably from contracts written as prose. Both are tractable and both are honest answers.

Beyond the hackathon

Nearest adjacencies

The same shape — a durable agent, a hard external deadline, evidence that decays — recurs across logistics.

  • Cargo claims and OS&D. Damage found at delivery triggers a workflow that gathers photos and inspection reports, determines liability against carrier terms, files within the statutory notice window, and pursues recovery over months.
  • Trade compliance. Classification, denied-party screening, and the long asynchronous loop with brokers and authorities. Here the audit trail is a regulatory requirement, not a nice-to-have.
  • Cold chain intervention. A temperature excursion in transit; the agent computes remaining shelf life and chooses between reroute, re-ice, expedite or divert — with compensation if the reroute fails.
  • Contract renewal intelligence. The learning store already knows which carriers' free time is nominally generous and effectively short at each port. That is direct procurement leverage. [23]

The general claim

Persistent Fleet is a demurrage product, but the pattern is not about demurrage. It is one durable agent per physical object, for as long as that object exists in the world. Anything with a lifespan measured in weeks and a cost of inattention measured in dollars fits the same architecture: reefer containers, aircraft components under airworthiness deadlines, pharmaceutical shipments, construction equipment on hire.

That is the claim for the final slide. The demurrage case proves it with real money; the pattern is what generalises.
